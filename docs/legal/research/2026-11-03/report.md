# Two live legal regimes already bind Smoke Ledger

Smoke Ledger is subject to binding data law in both of its markets today. EU users bring in the **GDPR in full** through Art 3(2), because EUR pricing and an EU country picker show the app targets them. The app's cigarette, craving, mood and note logs are almost certainly **Art 9 health data**, which requires explicit consent, an **EU representative under Art 27**, a DPIA, a record of processing and SCC-based contracts with the US processors. In India, the DPDP Act's main duties do **not** start until about **13 May 2027**. Until then the **IT Act s.43A and the SPDI Rules 2011** apply, and they treat health-condition data as sensitive: they require a published privacy policy, consent, a **named Grievance Officer who resolves complaints within one month**, and a documented security programme. The **CERT-In 6-hour incident-reporting direction** and **COTPA's tobacco-advertising ban** also apply now. The code audit shows the app already meets several hard requirements: opt-in analytics, opt-in AI chat that fails closed, an enforced 18+ gate, per-user row-level security, no ads, and in-app export, erase and delete. The same audit finds **ten places where the published privacy page promises more than the code delivers**. Three are high severity: erase leaves name, date of birth and gender in auth metadata; no retention purge exists for audit, error and usage logs; and raw error messages are stored unredacted. These gaps matter more than any missing clause, because regulators in comparable cases (Flo, GoodRx, BetterHelp) acted on broken promises, not just on the data sharing itself. The new privacy policy and terms should therefore be written against the code as it stands, and the code fixed where the promise is worth keeping.

> **This is research, not legal advice.** It synthesises research notes and a code audit (repo HEAD `5f672c0`). Several primary legal texts were not re-read in this session; those points are flagged below. A qualified lawyer in India and one in the EU should review the final policy and terms before you publish them.

## GDPR applies in full today, and the logs count as health data

The EU analysis starts with territorial scope. A non-EU controller falls under GDPR when it offers services to people in the Union, even for free. The EDPB lists the use of "a language or a currency generally used in one or more Member States" as a sign of that intent ([EDPB Guidelines 3/2018](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/sites/default/files/files/file1/edpb_guidelines_3_2018_territorial_scope_after_public_consultation_en_1.pdf)). Smoke Ledger's EUR currency and EU country picker meet that test. The same guidelines settle the representative question. The Art 27(2) exemption applies only when processing is **"occasional"**, meaning outside the regular course of the controller's activity, and does not involve large-scale special-category data. A daily-use tracker fails the first condition, and the conditions are cumulative. The operator therefore needs a **written mandate with an EU representative** in a Member State where its users live, and must name that representative in the privacy notice. Leaving the representative out of the notice is itself a transparency breach ([EDPB Guidelines 3/2018](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/sites/default/files/files/file1/edpb_guidelines_3_2018_territorial_scope_after_public_consultation_en_1.pdf)). A missing representative can be fined at the Art 83(4) tier, **up to EUR 10 million or 2% of turnover** ([GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)). Appointing a representative does not move liability onto the representative, and it does not give the operator the one-stop shop.

Next is whether the logs are health data. The CJEU reads "special categories" broadly. In *OT* (C-184/20) it held that data "liable indirectly to reveal sensitive information" stays inside the stricter Art 9 regime ([judgment text](https://pub-a0410b6281c64831b9c9612cf6920e67.r2.dev/celex-62020CJ0184.pdf)). In *Lindenapotheke* (C-21/23, 2024) it went further: online orders for non-prescription pharmacy products were health data because a link to the buyer's health could be inferred ([summary](https://www.thomashelbing.com/en/wissen/dsgvo-hub/rechtsprechung/1.4.31-eugh-lindenapotheke)). Cigarette counts, cravings, mood, triggers and quit patterns reveal much more than a pharmacy order. No regulator has ruled specifically on smoking trackers, so this classification is an inference. It is still the only safe one. The consequence is significant because **Art 9 has no "necessary for a contract" basis** (EDPB consent guidelines, para 99). The core ledger therefore needs **explicit consent**: an express statement such as "I hereby consent to the processing of my data", not "It is clear to me that my data will be processed" ([EDPB Guidelines 05/2020](https://www.dataprotection.ro/servlet/ViewDocument?id=2491)). The consent must be separate from accepting the terms, granular, recorded with its version, and as easy to withdraw as to give.

Several other duties apply to a one-person controller. The Art 30(5) exemption from keeping records of processing does not apply, because it falls away when processing is not occasional *or* includes special-category data ([GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)). A **DPIA** is effectively required: the app meets at least two of the nine WP248 criteria (sensitive data and innovative technology, here the LLM chat), and health data at scale is "considered as likely to result in a high risk" ([WP248 rev.01](https://ec.europa.eu/newsroom/article29/redirection/document/47711)). On transfers, the EDPB holds that an Indian controller collecting directly from EU users is not itself making a Chapter V transfer. Passing that data to a non-EEA processor is a transfer, so **Art 28 contracts and Chapter V safeguards** apply to Supabase, Vercel, Anthropic and the smaller vendors ([EDPB Guidelines 05/2021](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-02/edpb_guidelines_05-2021_interplay_between_the_application_of_art3-chapter_v_of_the_gdpr_v2_en_0.pdf)). Supabase's DPA relies on **SCC Modules 2 and 3, not the Data Privacy Framework**, and promises breach notice within 48 hours where feasible ([Supabase DPA](https://supabase.com/downloads/docs/Supabase+DPA+260317.pdf)). Anthropic's DPA forms part of its Commercial Terms, uses SCCs "to the extent required" and Irish law, and commits to breach notice within 48 hours ([Anthropic DPA](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/data-processing-addendum)). The DPF adequacy decision still stands. However, an appeal is pending before the CJEU (C-703/25 P) ([WilmerHale](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/blogs/wilmerhale-privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/20251201-european-court-of-justice-to-review-challenge-to-eu-us-data-privacy-framework)), and one secondary report says the EDPB Chair asked the Commission in July 2026 to reassess FTC independence after *Trump v. Slaughter* ([gblock.app](https://www.gblock.app/articles/edpb-eu-us-data-privacy-framework-review-slaughter-2026)). **Rely on SCCs plus a transfer impact assessment, and treat the DPF only as a bonus.**

Two operational duties differ from what an Indian operator might expect. First, breaches: a non-EU controller has no lead authority, so a notifiable breach must go to **"every supervisory authority for which affected data subjects reside"** within 72 hours ([EDPB Guidelines 9/2022](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-04/edpb_guidelines_202209_personal_data_breach_notification_v2.0_en.pdf)). That replaces the older advice to notify only the representative's authority. Second, device storage: ePrivacy Art 5(3) covers "cookies, local storage, WebSQL" whether or not the stored item is personal data ([EDPB Guidelines 2/2023](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2024-10/edpb_guidelines_202302_technical_scope_art_53_eprivacydirective_v2_en_0.pdf)). Session authentication cookies are exempt, but **persistent login cookies are exempt only when the user chooses "remember me"** ([WP194](https://ec.europa.eu/justice/article-29/documentation/opinion-recommendation/files/2012/wp194_en.pdf)). Smoke Ledger's design already fits that rule: the session cookie lasts only for the session unless the user selects remember-me ([code audit](../research_notes/Smoke%20Ledger%20data%20law%20compliance/implementation_evidence.md)). The weakest point is the chat transcript kept in localStorage. It holds health data and persists across sessions, so it should be disclosed and covered by the chat consent. Rights requests must be answered **free of charge within one month**, extendable by two months, and exports must be "structured, commonly used and machine-readable", which JSON satisfies ([GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)).

## India binds through 2011 rules until DPDP starts in May 2027

India's DPDP Act is often described as if it already applies in full. It does not. Rule 1 of the DPDP Rules, published **13 November 2025**, sets three stages. Board and institutional provisions started on publication. Consent Manager registration (Rule 4) starts after one year, around **13 November 2026**, and puts no duty on a small fiduciary. Rules 3, 5-16, 22 and 23, together with Act ss.3-17, start **"eighteen months after the date of publication"**, around **13 May 2027** ([DPDP Rules Gazette](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/11/53450e6e5dc0bfa85ebd78686cadad39.pdf); [AZB](https://www.azbpartners.com/bank/update-indias-digital-personal-data-protection-framework-comes-into-effect/)). Sources give 12, 13 or 14 May, because the instrument does not state the date directly. MeitY floated cutting the window to 12 months in January 2026 ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/technology/tech-news/meity-may-cut-compliance-timeline-for-key-dpdp-rules-to-12-months-126012201293_1.html)), but a tracker found no notified change as of mid-October 2026 ([dpdprules.org](https://dpdprules.org/blog/dpdp-18-months-to-12-months-proposal)). The DPDP Act covers an individual operator, because "person" includes an individual and the Act applies to processing in India ([DPDP Act](https://prsindia.org/files/bills_acts/acts_parliament/2023/Digital_Personal_Data_Protection_Act,_2023.pdf)). A fiduciary based in India therefore probably owes DPDP duties to its EU users as well. That is an interpretation, not settled law.

**Until May 2027, the binding Indian regime is the SPDI Rules 2011 under IT Act s.43A.** Rule 3 classes "physical, physiological and mental health condition" as sensitive personal data. Rule 4 requires a published privacy policy. Rule 5 requires consent before collection, notice of purpose and recipients, retention no longer than needed, a way to review and correct data, and a way to withdraw. Rule 5(9) requires a **Grievance Officer whose name and contact details are published and who resolves complaints "within one month"**. Rule 7 allows sensitive data to go abroad only to a recipient with equivalent protection, and only where needed for the contract or with consent. Rule 8 requires a documented security programme, with ISO 27001 given as an example ([SPDI Rules](https://PRSINDIA.org/files/bills_acts/bills_parliament/2011/IT_Rules_2011.pdf)). These rules reach an individual operator, because the s.43A definition of "body corporate" includes a "sole proprietorship ... engaged in commercial or professional activities" ([CERT-In FAQ Q25](https://www.cert-in.org.in/PDF/FAQs_on_CyberSecurityDirections_May2022.pdf)). The **CERT-In Directions** apply to the same entity and have been in force since 2022. They require reporting listed incidents, including data breaches and cloud attacks, **within 6 hours**; naming a point of contact; synchronising clocks to NTP; and keeping ICT logs on a 180-day rolling basis. Logs may be held abroad if they can be produced to CERT-In within a reasonable time ([CERT-In Directions](https://www.cert-in.org.in/PDF/CERT-In_Directions_70B_28.04.2022.pdf); [FAQs](https://www.cert-in.org.in/PDF/FAQs_on_CyberSecurityDirections_May2022.pdf)). **COTPA s.5** bans any advertisement that "directly or indirectly suggests or promotes" tobacco use, with penalties of up to two years' imprisonment ([COTPA](https://www.goatourism.gov.in/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/COTPA-2003.pdf)). For Smoke Ledger, that means no brand logos, pack imagery or promotional pricing in the app or store listings.

When DPDP starts, it brings five changes that affect drafting.

1. **Rule 3 notice.** The notice must be standalone and in plain language. It must itemise each data item and each purpose, link to the app, and explain how to withdraw consent, exercise rights and complain to the Data Protection Board. Under s.5(3), users must be able to read it in English **or any of the 22 Eighth Schedule languages** ([DPDP Rules Gazette](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/11/53450e6e5dc0bfa85ebd78686cadad39.pdf); [DPDP Act](https://prsindia.org/files/bills_acts/acts_parliament/2023/Digital_Personal_Data_Protection_Act,_2023.pdf)).
2. **Rule 14.** The operator must publish how to make rights requests, what identifier the user must provide, and a grievance process with a response time **not exceeding 90 days**. It must also offer a way to nominate someone to act for the user after death or incapacity.
3. **Rule 7 breach notice.** Every breach is notifiable, with no risk threshold. The operator must tell each affected user "without delay" with five specified items, and must send the Board an initial report and then a **detailed report within 72 hours**.
4. **One-year minimum retention.** Rule 6(1)(e) and Rule 8(3) require keeping personal data, traffic data and processing logs for **at least one year from processing**, then erasing them. The Rules' own example applies this "even if X deletes her account". This conflicts with the app's immediate deletion and with GDPR erasure. The workable reading is to delete content at once and keep only access and processing logs for 12 months, but no guidance confirms that reading yet.
5. **End of the SPDI regime.** s.44(2) removes s.43A when this stage starts, so the SPDI duties lapse as DPDP's begin. The ss.33 Schedule penalties then become enforceable, up to **Rs 250 crore** for security failures and **Rs 200 crore** for breach-notification failures, with no lower tier for small fiduciaries ([DPDP Act](https://prsindia.org/files/bills_acts/acts_parliament/2023/Digital_Personal_Data_Protection_Act,_2023.pdf)).

Several DPDP duties do not apply to Smoke Ledger. It is not a Significant Data Fiduciary, which requires government notification. It has no data-localisation duty and no 48-hour inactivity-erasure notice; the latter applies only to e-commerce, gaming and social media platforms with crore-scale user numbers. No country has been placed on the s.16 transfer blacklist, so US hosting remains lawful ([Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/india/data-protection/1764976/from-localisation-debates-to-a-negative-list-making-cross-border-data-transfers-work-under-indias-dpdp-act)). Until May 2027, any public claim to "comply with DPDP" is a claim of readiness, not of legal compliance. **"DPDP-aligned" is the accurate wording.**

## Consumer, AI and app-store rules shape the terms more than the policy

The terms of service face a different set of rules from the privacy policy. EU consumer law applies whatever the price. Under the **Unfair Contract Terms Directive**, terms must be plain and intelligible, ambiguities are read in the consumer's favour, and the Annex flags as unfair any term that excludes liability for death or personal injury, lets the trader change terms or the service without a stated valid reason, or hinders the consumer's access to court ([UCTD](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:31993L0013)). **Rome I Art 6** lets the operator choose Indian law, but an EU user keeps the mandatory protections of their home country ([Rome I](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32008R0593)). **Brussels Ia Arts 17-19** let EU consumers sue in their own courts, including against a non-EU trader, and invalidate a pre-dispute exclusive-forum clause against them ([Brussels Ia](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32012R1215)). An "exclusive jurisdiction of the courts of [Indian city]" clause is therefore unenforceable against EU users. It is fine for Indian users, provided it does not restrict rights under the Consumer Protection Act. That Act probably does not reach a free app, because s.2(42) excludes services "free of charge" ([iPleaders](https://blog.ipleaders.in/services-covered-consumer-protection-act-2019/)). The **new Product Liability Directive** treats software, including AI, as a product. Member States must transpose it by **9 December 2026**, and its liability cannot be excluded by contract ([PLD](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32024L2853)). Whether a free app run by one person counts as "commercial activity" under it has not been tested.

The AI chat brings in the AI Act. **Art 50(1)** requires telling users they are interacting with AI "at the latest at the time of the first interaction", unless that is obvious. It has applied since **2 August 2026**, and the Digital Omnibus did not delay it ([AI Act Art 50](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/); [Taylor Wessing](https://taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2026/05/the-eu-digital-omnibus-on-ai-what-the-political-deal-means)). Smoke Ledger offers the chat under its own brand, so it is probably the "provider" of that AI system, with Anthropic as the provider of the underlying model. The duty to mark AI output in machine-readable form (Art 50(2)) moves to **2 December 2026** for systems already on the market, if the omnibus is formally adopted; sources disagree on whether it has been ([William Fry](https://www.williamfry.com/knowledge/eu-ai-act-omnibus-deal-reached-postponed-deadlines-watermarking-compromise-and-the-nudificiation-prohibition/)). Medical-device rules turn on the **intended purpose shown in the marketing**, and a disclaimer cannot override that ([OpenRegulatory on MDCG 2019-11](https://openregulatory.com/mdcg/mdcg-2019-11)). Any claim that the app treats nicotine dependence would therefore risk turning it into a regulated device. **GDPR Art 22** does not apply to suggestions the user can ignore. They are still profiling, though, and WP29 says profiling should be disclosed even when Art 22 is not engaged ([WP260](https://ec.europa.eu/newsroom/article29/redirection/document/51025)).

The app stores enforce the most concrete wording. **Apple 5.1.2(i)** requires disclosing data shared "with third-party AI" and getting explicit permission first. **Apple 5.1.1(v)** requires in-app account deletion. **Apple 1.4.1** asks apps to remind users to check with a doctor. **Apple 1.4.3** prohibits apps that encourage tobacco use ([Apple Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). Google Play requires a Health apps declaration and, for non-regulated health apps, store-description text stating the app is **"not a medical device and does not diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any medical condition"** ([Google Play Health apps policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16679511)). Google also requires its AI-generated content policy to be followed for chat features ([Google AI-Generated Content](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/14094294)). India's dark-pattern guidelines list confirm-shaming, nagging and subscription traps ([JSA](https://www.jsalaw.com/newsletters-and-updates/ccpa-issues-guidelines-for-prevention-and-regulation-of-dark-patterns-2023/)). The EDPB's "Emotional Steering" pattern covers wording that makes users feel "anxious, guilty or punished" ([EDPB 03/2022](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-02/edpb_03-2022_guidelines_on_deceptive_design_patterns_in_social_media_platform_interfaces_v2_en_0.pdf)). Both match the product's existing design position: no streak-shaming, no alarm-red.

The most urgent work is not new clauses. It is closing the gap between the live privacy page and the code. The code audit is treated here as ground truth. It found these mismatches ([code audit](../research_notes/Smoke%20Ledger%20data%20law%20compliance/implementation_evidence.md)):

The audit also confirms what the code does well, and those points can be stated plainly as promises: **no ad networks and no sale of data; per-user RLS; analytics off until opt-in; AI chat opt-in and fail-closed; questions not stored server-side; web chat transcript cleared on sign-out, erase, delete and turn-off; erase wipes product tables; delete removes the auth identity and cascades (after a sign-in within 15 minutes); data hosted in us-east-2; 18+ enforced by a DOB gate and a database trigger** (`src/domain/age.ts`; `mig/20260616181124:144-156`).

The enforcement record shows why this order of work matters. The FTC's GoodRx case rested on a promise to "never share personal health information with advertisers" that the app broke. The order banned health-data advertising, required a published retention schedule and imposed a **$1.5 million** penalty ([FTC GoodRx](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/02/ftc-enforcement-action-bar-goodrx-sharing-consumers-sensitive-health-info-advertising)). BetterHelp paid **$7.8 million** on the same theory ([FTC BetterHelp](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/03/ftc-ban-betterhelp-revealing-consumers-data-including-sensitive-mental-health-information-facebook)). Neither regulator is Smoke Ledger's, but GDPR transparency and fairness turn on the same failure. Among cessation and depression apps, **33 of 36 sent data to third parties**, and 3 of those had said they would not ([Huckvale et al., JAMA Netw Open 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6481440/)). Smoke Ledger's code is cleaner than that benchmark. Its weakness is overstatement, not covert sharing. The ethics literature points the same way. The OECD's purpose-specification and use-limitation principles ([OECD](https://legalinstruments.oecd.org/public/doc/114/114.en.pdf)) and WHO's transparency and accountability principles ([WHO 2021](https://who.int/publications/i/item/9789240029200)) both favour a **short list of absolute, checkable promises**, each tied to a mechanism, over hedged wording of the kind found in Smoke Free's policy, which states two different retention periods ([Smoke Free policy](https://smokefreeapp.com/privacy-policy/)).

The AI chat flow needs especially precise wording. Anthropic's Commercial Terms state that it **"may not train models on Customer Content"** ([Anthropic Commercial Terms](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/commercial-terms)). It deletes API inputs and outputs **within 30 days** with exceptions, and keeps feedback-linked data for 5 years ([Anthropic privacy centre](https://privacy.claude.com/en/articles/7996866)). The policy should say that up to **200 recent entries**, including mood, trigger, craving and notes, plus earlier turns, go to Anthropic in the US when the user asks a question. It should also say that discreet mode replaces brand names with aliases and drops notes. "Never leaves your device" must not appear anywhere near the chat.

## Conclusion

Smoke Ledger's design choices are already better than its paperwork. It runs no tracking SDKs, keeps optional features opt-in and fail-closed, and enforces the age gate. Its legal exposure comes almost entirely from **unfixed promises and missing EU formalities**, not from risky data practices. That makes the work list unusually tractable. Fix the three high-severity code gaps, or remove the promises they break. Appoint an EU representative. Name a Grievance Officer. Publish a retention table that matches the code. Run one DPIA. The highest-value design decision is to write the new policy and consent flow to the **stricter of GDPR and DPDP now**: standalone itemised notices, per-purpose consent, in-app withdrawal, forced re-consent on material changes. Done that way, the May 2027 DPDP start becomes a translation and contact-detail exercise rather than a second re-consent campaign for live users.

The one real legal tension to resolve with counsel is retention. **GDPR pushes towards erasing everything on request. DPDP Rule 8(3) will require keeping processing logs for a year, even after deletion.** A design that erases content immediately, keeps only minimal access and processing logs for 12 months in restricted storage, then purges them, and discloses this, is the most defensible reconciliation. It also forces the log-purge job the code currently lacks.

## 3. Decisions only the owner can make

1. **EU representative, or stop targeting the EU.** You can appoint a commercial representative service or an EU-based individual with a written mandate, in the Member State with the most users. The EDPB recommends that state. **The research found no sourced pricing**, so get quotes before deciding. The alternative is to remove the targeting signals (EUR currency, EU country picker, EU marketing). That ends Art 3(2)(a) targeting going forward but does not clearly cover existing EU users' data. Decide before anything else, because the choice changes the notice, the breach runbook and the currency feature.
2. **Grievance Officer and contact person.** SPDI requires a published **name** and contact details. As an individual operator you must decide whose name appears, whether to use a dedicated mailbox rather than `support@`, whether to publish a postal address, and what response time to promise. A 30-day resolution target satisfies both SPDI's one month and DPDP's 90-day cap.
3. **Governing law and forum.** Recommended: Indian law and the courts of your city as the default, plus an explicit clause that EU consumers keep their home-country mandatory protections and courts. Decide whether to offer arbitration for Indian users. It is optional, and it must not bind consumers.
4. **Hosting region.** Keep Supabase in us-east-2 (SCCs + TIA), or move the project to an EU region. A move removes the largest EU transfer, but Vercel and Anthropic would still be in the US.
5. **How much data the AI chat sends.** Decide whether to keep 200 entries plus earlier turns, cut to aggregates, drop notes by default, show a pre-send preview, or ask Anthropic about zero data retention.
6. **Retention numbers.** Set a period for each table (`audit_events`, `error_events`, `usage_events`, backups/PITR, inactive accounts) and decide how to reconcile immediate erasure with DPDP's 1-year log minimum.
7. **Fix or retract each broken promise.** For audit items 1-10, decide whether to change the code or soften the wording. Items 1-3 should be fixed.
8. **Re-consent policy.** Reverse the spec-08 decision so that material version bumps force re-acceptance, and decide what counts as "material".
9. **Identity-field correction.** Make name, DOB and gender editable (perhaps with DOB re-checked against the 18+ rule), or document why not.
10. **Chat transcript persistence.** Keep it in localStorage under the chat consent, or move it to session-only storage.
11. **Languages.** Plan translations into the Eighth Schedule languages before May 2027, and decide which EU languages the notice will be offered in.
12. **CERT-In registration.** Decide whether to treat yourself as covered, which is the conservative reading, and register a point of contact.

## 4. Uncertainties and items to verify with a lawyer

- **DPDP start date.** It is computed, not stated: 12, 13 or 14 May 2027. A proposal to shorten the window to 12 months was not confirmed as notified, and no eGazette check was possible after February 2026. The content of the February 2026 Rules corrigendum (G.S.R. 113(E)) was not retrieved.
- **Whether smoking logs are "health" data.** No regulator or court has classified tobacco-use logs, either as SPDI "health condition" data in India or as Art 9 data in the EU. Both classifications are inference, and the conservative one is used here.
- **SPDI "consent in writing".** Whether in-app click consent satisfies Rule 5(1) ("letter or Fax or email") has not been decided.
- **DPDP Rule 8(3) scope.** Whether the 1-year retention covers full entry content or only access and traffic logs, and how it fits with s.12(3), s.8(7) and GDPR Art 17.
- **DPDP for EU users.** Whether DPDP duties apply to EU-resident users of an India-based fiduciary is interpretation (probably yes).
- **CERT-In coverage.** Whether the Directions cover an individual operator, given the FAQ's "individual citizens are not covered" line.
- **DPF status.** The Latombe appeal (C-703/25 P) is pending. The July 2026 EDPB letter rests on one secondary source. Anthropic's, Vercel's and Expo's DPF certification and DPA terms were not checked against primary sources.
- **Anthropic retention.** Its official page says 30 days; secondary claims say 7 days since September 2025. Also confirm the Commercial Terms and DPA cover your API account.
- **AI Act Art 50(2).** Formal adoption of the AI Omnibus and the 2 December 2026 date are unconfirmed, as is whether Anthropic's output marking is enough.
- **India's 2026 IT Rules amendment on "synthetically generated information".** Not read; it may impose labelling duties on the AI chat. High priority.
- **Intermediary status.** Whether the app is an "intermediary" under IT Act s.2(1)(w) and the IT Rules 2021 grievance timelines (24 h / 15 days).
- **Consumer law applicability.** Whether the Consumer Protection Act 2019 and the dark-pattern guidelines bind a free app; whether the DCD and CRD apply where personal data is used only to provide the service; whether the PLD reaches a free app from a one-person operator.
- **ePrivacy and localStorage.** Whether a purely first-party localStorage write counts as "storage" under Art 5(3), and national positions (CNIL, TDDDG s.25) on UI flags and chat history.
- **National EU variations.** Member-State DPIA lists, Art 8 age thresholds and Art 9(4) extra conditions for health data were not tabulated.
- **COTPA online.** Whether COTPA's "advertisement" reaches app content, and whether the 2020 amendment bill was enacted.
- **EU ODR platform and Digital Fairness Act.** The ODR repeal (recalled, not sourced) and the Digital Fairness Act's status, before you drop or add clauses.
- **Mandatory EU breach notice in practice.** Each national authority's breach-form languages and channels.
- **EU representative.** Market pricing and service terms were not researched.
- **Data Protection Board.** Whether its appointments and complaint portal are operational, so the policy's complaint route works.
