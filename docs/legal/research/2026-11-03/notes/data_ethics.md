# Data ethics and practical standards for a private smoking-habit tracking app (Smoke Ledger), as of November 2026

Scope: ethics and best practice beyond legal minimums. Legal duties (GDPR, DPDP, consumer law) are covered by other researchers. Research limits: WebFetch failed or returned partial content for several primary sources (WHO full text, ICO, India CCPA annexure, NHS app page, QuitGenius not found at all). Where a claim rests on a search-engine summary rather than a fetched primary page, the finding says so.

## 1. Ethical frameworks for mHealth/AI-for-health (WHO, OECD, Montreal/Toronto) and how they map to this app

### Takeaway
WHO's 2021 guidance names six principles (protect autonomy, promote safety/well-being, ensure transparency, foster accountability, ensure equity, promote responsive/sustainable tools); the OECD's eight fair-information principles supply the classic data-minimisation/purpose-limitation vocabulary; the Montreal Declaration adds explicit "autonomy" and "privacy and intimacy" principles. Together they map cleanly to a private-journal app: user-controlled data, collect little, use only for the stated purpose, say plainly what leaves the device, and have a named accountable owner.

### Cited Findings
- WHO "Ethics and governance of artificial intelligence for health" (June 2021) sets out six principles: protecting autonomy; promoting human safety and well-being; ensuring transparency; fostering accountability; ensuring equity; promoting tools that are responsive and sustainable. The two sources I fetched list the names only, without definitions — [WHO publication page](https://who.int/publications/i/item/9789240029200); [Genetics and Society summary](https://www.geneticsandsociety.org/article/who-outlines-principles-ethics-health-ai) (names listed, no definitions given on that page).
- WHO guidance on large multi-modal models (LMMs, i.e. generative AI such as chat assistants): the WHO IRIS record is dated 25 March 2025 on the WHO page (98 pages, ISBN 978-92-4-008475-9); WHO's own news release is dated 18 January 2024. The page states "it is not yet proven whether LMMs can accomplish a wide range of tasks and purposes." [WHO LMM publication page](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240084759); [WHO news release, 18 Jan 2024](https://www.who.int/news/item/18-01-2024-who-releases-ai-ethics-and-governance-guidance-for-large-multi-modal-models). Note the date discrepancy (the January 2024 release vs. a 2025 listing on the publication page); I did not resolve whether 2025 is a re-publication.
- The LMM guidance contains over 40 recommendations; per secondary summaries it urges developers to involve potential users and affected people early in a transparent design process, flags cybersecurity risks to patient information, and advises governments to require post-release auditing and impact assessments (including data protection and human rights) for large-scale deployments — [search summary of WHO LMM guidance, via HealthManagement.org/WHO news](https://www.who.int/news/item/18-01-2024-who-releases-ai-ethics-and-governance-guidance-for-large-multi-modal-models) (secondary; verify exact wording in the 98-page PDF).
- OECD Privacy Guidelines (1980, revised 2013): eight principles — collection limitation, data quality, purpose specification, use limitation, security safeguards, openness, individual participation, accountability. Not binding but a baseline for many laws including GDPR. Purpose specification = purposes identified at or before collection; use limitation = no use/disclosure beyond stated purposes without consent or legal authority; accountability = controller answers for compliance. 2013 revision added privacy management programmes and breach notification — [OECD legal instrument (PDF)](https://legalinstruments.oecd.org/public/doc/114/114.en.pdf); [Piwik PRO summary](https://piwik.pro/blog/oecd-guidelines-8-privacy-principles-to-live-by/) (secondary).
- Montréal Declaration (Université de Montréal, December 2018): "AIS must be developed and used with respect for people's autonomy, and with the goal of increasing people's control over their lives and their surroundings"; "Privacy and intimacy must be protected from intrusion by AIS and by data-acquisition and archiving systems." — [Montréal Declaration](https://montrealdeclaration-responsibleai.com/the-declaration/) (quoted via search summary; confirm on the official page before quoting publicly).
- ICO (UK) reference practice: a review of period/fertility apps urged developers to prioritise privacy and be more transparent; ICO recommends privacy by design and data minimisation for digital health — [ICO app-developer news, Feb 2024 (page returned 403 to me; content via search summary)](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2024/02/ico-urges-all-app-developers-to-prioritise-privacy); [UKAuthority on ICO digital health](https://www.ukauthority.com/articles/ico-advocates-privacy-by-design-in-digital-health/).
- EDPB: fairness requires data-processing information and options be "provided in an objective and neutral way, avoiding any deceptive or manipulative language or design"; data protection by default means only data necessary for each purpose is processed by default — [EDPB Guidelines 03/2022 v2.0 (PDF), fetched and text-extracted](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-02/edpb_03-2022_guidelines_on_deceptive_design_patterns_in_social_media_platform_interfaces_v2_en_0.pdf).

### Inferences
- Mapping to Smoke Ledger: autonomy -> user decides what is logged, opt-in AI chat, opt-in analytics, discreet mode, deletion; beneficence/non-maleficence -> no guilt framing, no medical advice, no coaching nudges that could cause harm; transparency -> plain account of what leaves the device (AI chat to Anthropic, analytics in-house); data minimisation/purpose limitation -> data used only to show the user their own journal; accountability -> a named individual operator and a contact route (appropriate to a solo operator; WHO's accountability principle is about an identifiable responsible party).
- WHO's equity/inclusiveness principle maps weakly to this app (small solo-run journal); relevant remainder is accessibility and language, and not excluding people on the basis of country (India/EU).
- The Toronto Declaration (human rights/non-discrimination in ML) is less directly relevant; the relevant mapping is non-discrimination risk if data reached insurers/employers (see section 2).

### Gaps
- WHO's own definitions of the six principles and the LMM guidance's exact recommendations were not retrievable (only names and secondary summaries). Writers should check the 2021 PDF and the LMM PDF before attributing specific wording.
- The Toronto Declaration (2018, Amnesty/Access Now) was not located in search; search results confused it with an unrelated "Tech for Good" declaration. Not cited.
- No WHO "digital health guidelines" (e.g. the 2019 WHO guideline on digital interventions for health system strengthening) were researched in this pass.

## 2. Stigma, sensitivity and how cessation apps word their privacy promises

### Takeaway
Real-world evidence shows sensitive health apps routinely broke their own privacy promises (Flo, GoodRx, BetterHelp) and that most smoking-cessation apps shared data with third parties (Huckvale et al. 2019). The good-practice contrast is simple, verifiable, absolute promises ("never share with advertisers/insurers") rather than the hedged, internally contradictory wording seen in Smoke Free's policy.

### Cited Findings
- Flo Health (FTC, Jan 2021, finalised June 2021): period/fertility app shared health data (e.g. pregnancy "app events") with Facebook, Google's analytics, Google Fabric, AppsFlyer and Flurry despite privacy promises; received 300+ user complaints and reportedly only stopped after a news article in Feb 2019. Order: independent privacy review, consent before sharing health data, notify affected users, instruct third parties to destroy the data. Commissioners Chopra and Slaughter argued Health Breach Notification Rule charges should also have been brought — [FTC Flo final-order release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2021/06/ftc-finalizes-order-flo-health-fertility-tracking-app-shared-sensitive-health-data-facebook-google) (via search summary; direct fetch of the January release returned 404).
- GoodRx (FTC, 1 Feb 2023): "deceptively promised its users that it would never share personal health information with advertisers or other third parties"; shared prescription/condition data with Facebook, Google, Criteo; displayed a seal falsely suggesting HIPAA compliance; claimed Digital Advertising Alliance compliance; failed to notify of unauthorised disclosures. Proposed order: permanent ban on sharing health data for advertising; affirmative express consent for other sharing and a ban on "manipulative designs, known as dark patterns" in obtaining that consent; third-party deletion; published retention schedule; privacy programme; $1.5 million civil penalty. First action under the Health Breach Notification Rule — [FTC GoodRx release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/02/ftc-enforcement-action-bar-goodrx-sharing-consumers-sensitive-health-info-advertising).
- BetterHelp (FTC, 2 Mar 2023): promised to limit use/disclosure of health data but shared email, IP address and health-questionnaire information with Facebook, Snapchat, Criteo and Pinterest for advertising; lacked affirmative express consent and any limits on third-party use; in 2020 falsely denied news reports of sharing. FTC: "BetterHelp betrayed consumers' most personal health information for profit." Order: advertising ban, consent, comprehensive privacy programme, third-party deletion, retention schedule, $7.8 million for partial refunds — [FTC BetterHelp release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/03/ftc-ban-betterhelp-revealing-consumers-data-including-sensitive-mental-health-information-facebook).
- Peer-reviewed evidence on cessation apps: Huckvale, Torous, Larsen (JAMA Network Open, 2019), 36 top apps for depression and smoking cessation (data from early 2018): 25 of 36 (69%) had a privacy policy; 33 of 36 (92%) transmitted data to a third party (mostly Google/Facebook services); of these 9 had no privacy policy, 5 did not mention transmission, 3 stated transmission would not occur. Mostly advertising/device identifiers, no obvious names/medical data observed. Authors: assume data will be shared with commercial entities and do not rely on privacy-policy disclosures — [PMC6481440](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6481440/) (figures via search summary; secondary counts for Google/Facebook recipients vary).
- Mozilla *Privacy Not Included, 2022 mental-health/prayer app review: 28 of 32 apps got the warning label; 25 failed minimum security standards; at least eight accepted weak passwords (e.g. "1"); flagged vague, messy privacy policies, sharing with third parties and collection of chat transcripts; only PTSD Coach and Wysa judged trustworthy — [Mozilla Foundation](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/en/blog/top-mental-health-and-prayer-apps-fail-spectacularly-at-privacy-security/). I found no Mozilla review of a quit-smoking app.
- Smoke Free (smokefreeapp.com) privacy policy as an example of hedged wording: collects names, emails, age; says PII "sometimes" shared with third parties "like our partners at UCL" (legal compliance, safety, scientific purposes); users "may see advertisements on the Platform"; marketing cookie category; erasure possible but "we may need to keep some of the personal data" for up to six years; summary says data not retained longer than two years after last log-in but Section 5 says "for so long as you have a registered account"; anonymised data "indefinitely"; the same policy also says "No such personally identifiable information about your smoking status is ever shared outside of the Platform," which sits uneasily with the UCL sharing clause — i.e. its statements are in tension. It does not mention selling — [Smoke Free privacy policy](https://smokefreeapp.com/privacy-policy/). (My reading of the tension is an observation on the fetched text, not a finding of wrongdoing.)
- Bitdefender (2025-ish blog, secondary) reports quit-smoking/sobriety apps can collect cravings, moods, journal entries and device data, cites a Play Store listing for "Quit Tracker" saying it may collect/share location and financial info, and cites a 2023 TechCrunch investigation that alcohol-recovery apps (Tempest, Momentum) shared sensitive data with third parties — [Bitdefender Hot for Security](https://bitdefender.com/en-au/blog/hotforsecurity/mindful-or-monitored-what-quit-smoking-and-sobriety-apps-know-about-you-and-why-it-matters). Secondary source; reports no known breaches of quit-smoking apps.

### Inferences
- Common failure mode in every FTC case: a promise in the privacy policy/marketing contradicted by SDK/pixel behaviour. The lesson for Smoke Ledger is to make each promise mechanically true (no third-party ad/analytics SDKs on logged data) and verifiable (e.g. a published list of every outbound network destination).
- Harm vectors for smoking data are plausible but I found no cited source quantifying insurer/employer misuse of smoking-app data; the sensitivity argument rests on the FTC cases above (health data used for advertising) and general stigma. Treat insurer/employer/family harm as reasoned risk, not evidenced incident. India/EU insurers do price-load smokers in general, but I did not source this.
- Good wording pattern to emulate: absolute, short, testable ("We never sell your data. We run no advertising. We share nothing with insurers or employers."), no "sometimes/may/partners" hedges, and no contradictory retention statements.

### Gaps
- NHS Quit Smoking app privacy wording: the NHS Apps Library page returned HTTP 410 Gone; not verified. QuitGenius: no source found. Only Smoke Free was analysed.
- No sourced evidence on actual insurer/employer purchases of cessation-app data.
- FTC Flo January 2021 release URL returned 404; claims rely on the June 2021 release listing found via search.

## 3. AI-specific ethics (disclosure, no training, no manipulation, what leaves the device)

### Takeaway
Anthropic's commercial terms bar training on customer content, and API data is auto-deleted from Anthropic's backend within 30 days (with exceptions), so a "not used to train models" promise is supportable for an API-integrated chat — but "your notes leave your device when you use chat" must be stated plainly, and chat feedback features can extend retention to 5 years. WHO/EDPB-style expectations point to clear AI disclosure and to no emotionally manipulative design.

### Cited Findings
- Anthropic Commercial Terms, Section B: "Anthropic may not train models on Customer Content from Services." Customer Content is the customer's Confidential Information (E.1); confidential information is destroyed on request except where retained for law or in automated back-ups (E.4); data is processed under the Data Processing Addendum (C) — [Anthropic Commercial Terms](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/commercial-terms).
- Anthropic privacy centre: "we automatically delete inputs and outputs on our backend within 30 days of receipt or generation," with exceptions (retention under customer control such as Files API, agreed terms such as zero-data-retention, Usage Policy enforcement, legal requirements); data tied to feedback (thumbs up/down, bug reports) is retained 5 years — [Anthropic privacy centre, retention article](https://privacy.claude.com/en/articles/7996866).
- Conflict: third-party blogs claim API log retention was cut to 7 days in September 2025; I could not confirm on Anthropic's own pages. Use the official 30-day figure and re-check before publishing — [developersdigest summary](https://www.developersdigest.tech/models/data-retention/anthropic) (secondary, unverified).
- EDPB defines Emotional Steering as wording or visuals conveying information "in either a highly positive outlook, making users feel good, safe or rewarded, or a highly negative one, making users feel anxious, guilty or punished," with stronger effect on vulnerable users — [EDPB 03/2022](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-02/edpb_03-2022_guidelines_on_deceptive_design_patterns_in_social_media_platform_interfaces_v2_en_0.pdf). (Directed at social media platforms, but its principle is general.)
- WHO LMM guidance (per secondary summary) flags LMM risks around data quality, bias and cybersecurity of patient information and recommends impact assessments for large-scale deployments — see section 1 sources. WHO's LMM page also states effectiveness across health tasks is "not yet proven."

### Inferences
- Commitments supported by sources: (a) AI chat is opt-in and off by default; (b) label every AI reply as AI-generated; (c) state the specific third party (Anthropic), what is sent (the user's own relevant entries/aggregates), that it is not used to train models under the commercial terms, and the deletion window (30 days per Anthropic, subject to confirmation); (d) the AI describes the user's own data and does not diagnose, prescribe, or advise on medication/NRT; points to a clinician or helpline for medical questions; (e) tone: observational, non-judgmental, no streak/guilt language; (f) allow deleting chat history in-app, noting that Anthropic's backend copy ages out by its schedule.
- Do not promise "never leaves your device" for anything routed through the chat; promise "leaves only when you start a chat" and show a pre-send disclosure.
- WHO's "not yet proven" caution supports an explicit "this is a narrator, not a coach or clinician" statement.

### Gaps
- Anthropic DPA details (sub-processors, location of processing, India/EU transfer mechanism) not examined; another researcher may cover cross-border transfer.
- Could not retrieve WHO LMM text on disclosure to users that content is AI-generated; general expectation inferred, not cited.

## 4. Dark patterns a quit app must avoid (EDPB 03/2022, India CCPA 2023)

### Takeaway
EDPB's taxonomy (six categories, 17 types) and India's 13 named dark patterns converge on the behaviour-change-app risks: guilt/shame messaging, confirmshaming on opt-outs, nagging/continuous prompting for consent, hard-to-cancel/delete flows, and pre-ticked privacy-invasive defaults.

### Cited Findings
- EDPB 03/2022 v2.0 (adopted 14 Feb 2023) categories and types: Overloading (Continuous prompting, Privacy Maze, Too many options); Skipping (Deceptive snugness, Look over there); Stirring (Emotional Steering, Hidden in plain sight); Obstructing (Dead end, Longer than necessary, Misleading action); Fickle (Lacking hierarchy, Decontextualising, Inconsistent interface, Language discontinuity); Left in the dark (Conflicting information, Ambiguous wording or information) — [EDPB 03/2022 PDF](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2023-02/edpb_03-2022_guidelines_on_deceptive_design_patterns_in_social_media_platform_interfaces_v2_en_0.pdf).
- EDPB on Continuous prompting: repeatedly asking users who declined consent (its example is a recurring personalised-ads box) is likely to "fatigue users into consenting," and conflicts with freely-given consent and with withdrawing consent "as easily as giving" it. On Deceptive snugness: most data-invasive options enabled by default conflicts with data protection by default (Art. 25(2) GDPR) — same PDF.
- EDPB on account deletion/exit: best practice is a shortcut link to account deletion in the user account; keep steps to change a data setting "as low as possible"; excessive steps infringe fairness. Also recommended: shortcuts, a "data protection directory" page, coherent wording, definitions, examples, explaining consequences, cross-device consistency; EDPB recommends interdisciplinary teams (designers, DPO, decision-makers) — same PDF.
- India: Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Dark Patterns, 2023, notified 30 Nov 2023 under Section 18 of the Consumer Protection Act 2019; 13 patterns in Annexure 1 (list is non-exhaustive; contraventions attract penalty under the Act); includes False Urgency, Confirm Shaming, Drip Pricing, Interface Interference, Subscription Trap, Bait and Switch and Nagging. Confirm-shaming example: declining flight insurance prompts "I will stay unsecured." Applies to platforms offering goods or services in India including advertisers and sellers — [JSA law firm summary](https://www.jsalaw.com/newsletters-and-updates/ccpa-issues-guidelines-for-prevention-and-regulation-of-dark-patterns-2023/) (page returned 403 to my fetch; content via search summary); [Inc42](https://inc42.com/buzz/govt-issues-list-of-13-dark-patterns-plaguing-ecommerce-websites). The official gazette PDF (consumeraffairs.nic.in) was unreachable; verify definitions there.
- FTC GoodRx order bans "manipulative designs, known as dark patterns" when obtaining consent for health-data sharing — [FTC GoodRx release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/02/ftc-enforcement-action-bar-goodrx-sharing-consumers-sensitive-health-info-advertising).

### Inferences
- Concrete patterns a quit/habit app must avoid: guilt or shame copy on a relapse or high-count day (EDPB Emotional Steering "anxious, guilty or punished"); confirmshaming on opt-outs ("No, I don't care about my health"); re-asking for AI chat or analytics opt-in after a "no" (Continuous prompting/Nagging); streak-loss warnings or push notifications engineered to bring the user back; opt-in toggles pre-ticked (Deceptive snugness); burying delete/export (Dead end, Privacy Maze); celebratory/reward framing that varies with consent choice; false urgency about quitting; "Misleading action" such as a "Done" button that also consents.
- Concrete counter-practices: symmetric Accept/Decline buttons in neutral wording; one ask per feature, remembered; in-context shortcut to the privacy page from each data-related screen; delete-account within two taps from Settings; notification defaults off.
- The app's own design stance (calm, non-judgmental, no gamification) already matches these standards; the ethics statement can cite it as policy.

### Gaps
- Official CCPA Annexure 1 wording (e.g. exact definition of Nagging and Subscription trap) not retrieved; definitions above for India rely on law-firm and news summaries.
- No source found assessing whether the CCPA guidelines apply to a free, non-commercial journal app (applicability is a legal question for others).

## 5. Privacy-by-design practices for sensitive personal logs

### Takeaway
The sources agree on fewer data collected by default, protective defaults, easy exit/deletion, retention schedules, third-party deletion and independent review; the FTC orders show what regulators demand after a failure, so a solo operator can adopt them proactively. Specific technical claims (local-first, encryption) were not researched in depth in this pass.

### Cited Findings
- FTC orders (GoodRx, BetterHelp) require a published or documented data retention schedule, a comprehensive privacy programme, third-party deletion, and affirmative express consent for sharing — FTC releases linked in section 2.
- FTC Flo order required an independent privacy review and user notification of past disclosures — FTC Flo release linked in section 2.
- Mozilla found weak password acceptance (e.g. one character) and unclear security-update processes across mental-health apps; it treats strong passwords and update/vulnerability management as minimum security standards — [Mozilla Foundation](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/en/blog/top-mental-health-and-prayer-apps-fail-spectacularly-at-privacy-security/).
- ICO guidance direction: privacy by design, data minimisation, privacy-enhancing technologies; a UK law-firm guide suggests defaulting to the most protective privacy setting and limiting staff access to sensitive records — [UKAuthority](https://www.ukauthority.com/articles/ico-advocates-privacy-by-design-in-digital-health/); [Sprintlaw](https://sprintlaw.co.uk/articles/data-protection-healthtech-apps/) (low-authority secondary).
- EDPB best practices relevant to exit: account-deletion shortcut, minimal steps, explaining consequences before the user acts, data protection directory — EDPB PDF in section 4.
- OECD security safeguards, individual participation and accountability principles — see section 1.
- Smoke Free's policy (section 2) demonstrates the retention-contradiction problem: two different retention statements in one policy.

### Inferences
- Practices consistent with the sources and suitable for Smoke Ledger: collect only what the journal needs (no contacts, location, advertising IDs); privacy-protective defaults (discreet mode available, analytics and AI off until opted in); strong-password minimum and a stated process for security updates; one clear retention rule per data type published and matched to the code; deletion that is finite and explained ("what is erased now, what ages out of backups and by when"); full export of the user's own data in a common format; a short, honest incident/breach commitment (tell affected users promptly with what happened and what to do) — breach-notice practice is derived from FTC Health Breach Notification Rule expectations (GoodRx) and OECD 2013 revision, not from a source on solo-operator best practice; adult-only statement and no knowing collection from minors.
- An audit trail can be offered as a user-visible record of consent changes and export/delete actions; I did not find a standard calling for this in the ethics literature, so frame as optional good practice.
- Local-first/offline storage and encryption at rest/in transit were not researched with sources; treat as design choices to be described accurately rather than as claims drawn from this research.

### Gaps
- No sourced standard specifically for local-first storage, client-side encryption, or audit-trail logging in health journal apps.
- Kids exclusion: no source gathered (minimum age, age-gating practice). Legal minimum ages differ across India (DPDP, under-18 verifiable parental consent) and EU member states; another researcher should cover. Ethics point only: state "for adults 18+" and do not target minors.
- Breach-transparency practice for individual operators: no source gathered.

## 6. What a short, honest "Our data promises" section looks like, and keeping it verifiable

### Takeaway
Evidence from the FTC cases and Smoke Free's policy points to the same rule: promises should be few, absolute, plain-language, and each tied to a specific mechanism the user or an outsider can check; any promise that cannot be mechanically kept should be softened or removed because deceptive promises, not just sharing, were the FTC's core charge.

### Cited Findings
- Core FTC theory in GoodRx/BetterHelp/Flo: a stated promise (never share health data with advertisers; limit use) contradicted by actual practice. GoodRx also faced a charge for a false compliance seal (HIPAA) — never display trust seals or certifications not actually held — FTC releases in section 2.
- Hedged phrasing is a documented weakness: Mozilla criticised "incredibly vague and messy privacy policies" — [Mozilla](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/en/blog/top-mental-health-and-prayer-apps-fail-spectacularly-at-privacy-security/); Smoke Free's policy uses "sometimes" and "may" and contains inconsistent retention statements — [Smoke Free policy](https://smokefreeapp.com/privacy-policy/).
- Huckvale et al.: the authors advise users not to rely on privacy-policy disclosures; verification beyond words matters — [PMC6481440](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6481440/).
- EDPB: data protection information must be easily noticed and findable ("Hidden in plain sight" and "Dead end" are deceptive patterns); layered notices and shortcuts from each relevant screen are best practice — EDPB PDF in section 4.
- Wysa and PTSD Coach were the only two apps Mozilla found trustworthy in its 2022 review, a positive reference class — Mozilla link above.

### Inferences
- Draft shape (each line with a mechanism, to be confirmed as true by the operator before publishing):
  1. "Your log is yours. Nobody else sees it." -> row-level access control; operator access policy stated (what, when, why).
  2. "We never sell your data and run no advertising." -> no ad SDKs; published list of every outbound domain.
  3. "We never share with insurers, employers, family, or government unless compelled by law, and we will say so if allowed." -> no data-sharing integrations; a statement on how legal requests are handled (needs legal input).
  4. "Analytics are off until you opt in, count events not content, and never include notes, amounts or moods." -> event schema published; toggle in Settings.
  5. "AI chat is off until you turn it on. When on, the relevant part of your data is sent to Anthropic to write the reply. Anthropic does not train on it. Replies are AI-generated and are not medical advice." -> link to Anthropic commercial terms; label on each message; pre-send disclosure; Anthropic's deletion window stated as 30 days (confirm).
  6. "Discreet mode masks figures on screen; it is not encryption." -> state the limit honestly (the app's own memory notes mask at every render site).
  7. "Delete and export any time, in a few taps. Deletion is final; here is what happens to backups and when." -> in-app buttons; a stated backup-expiry period.
  8. "We keep data only as long as you have an account (or a stated period)." -> single retention table; match to code.
  9. "If something goes wrong, we tell affected users promptly." -> contact route and commitment.
  10. "For adults 18+." and "I am one person running this; here is how to reach me." -> named operator and contact email.
- Verification ladder: promise -> mechanism -> evidence (code path, settings screen, outbound domain list, change log). Keep a dated "what changed" log for the promises page so that edits are visible (this is an inference from accountability principles, not a cited standard).
- State what you do not promise (e.g. perfect security, no future changes) to avoid overstatement; FTC cases turn on overstatement.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or regulator-published template for a "data promises" section was found; the drafts above are synthesised by me.
- Did not find a source assessing how users read or trust such promise lists (e.g. comprehension studies); deliberately not asserted.
